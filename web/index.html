    Why length wins

    The search space is RL: pool size to the power of length. Adding one character multiplies the work by R, while adding a character set only grows R. A 4-digit PIN has 10,000 combinations; 12 mixed characters have about 5 × 1023.

    Why dictionary attacks work, and how to defend

    • People pick memorable words. Real leaked lists like RockYou hold 14 million passwords from actual breaches.
    • Rules make it worse. Attackers try variations such as dragon → Dr@g0n2024!, so simple substitutions don't help much.
    • Defenses: rate limiting and lockout, slow salted hashing (Argon2id, bcrypt, PBKDF2), and multi-factor authentication.

    Module 5

    Password Hashing Lab

    SHA-256 turns any input into a fixed 64-character fingerprint. See that it's deterministic, that tiny changes scramble it, and why salt matters.

    —

    SHA-256 alone is not enough for passwords

    • It's built to be fast. A modern GPU computes over 10 billion SHA-256 hashes per second, great for file integrity, bad for passwords.
    • Unsalted hashes leak. Identical passwords give identical hashes, so precomputed rainbow tables crack them in bulk.
    • Use a password KDF. Argon2id, bcrypt or PBKDF2 with a unique salt and a high cost factor.

    What just happened

    The tool took a candidate like password, computed SHA-256("password"), and compared it with the target. When the two strings are equal, the plaintext is known. Nothing was decrypted. That's why a password found in any wordlist is unsafe even when it's hashed.

      Lockouts vs. denial of service

      • Why lock at all? Without limits, an online attacker can try thousands of guesses.
      • The catch: an attacker can lock real users out on purpose by failing three times.
      • Modern fixes: exponential backoff, CAPTCHA after a few failures, and risk-based checks on unfamiliar devices.

        Read these numbers with care

        • Theoretical keyspace. This assumes every combination is tried. Length grows the space exponentially.
        • Real attacks are often faster. Attackers try dictionary words, leaked passwords and patterns (capital first letter, year at the end) first.
        • Hashing choice matters. Slow KDFs cut GPU speed from billions to around a thousand guesses per second.
